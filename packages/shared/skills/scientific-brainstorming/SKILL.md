# Scientific brainstorming

The stage before there is anything to write: deciding what is worth investigating, and
being honest about how little is known yet.

Adapted from `scientific-brainstorming` in K-Dense-AI/scientific-agent-skills (MIT).
See NOTICE.

## Keep five things separate, and label them

Most bad research thinking comes from one of these quietly turning into another:

- **idea** — a direction someone might take;
- **assumption** — something being taken as true without checking;
- **prediction** — what should be observed if the idea holds;
- **located evidence** — something actually found in a source, with the source named;
- **decision** — what the writer has settled on, and why.

Say which one you are producing. An idea presented in the grammar of evidence is the most
expensive mistake at this stage, because everything downstream is built on it.

## Generate before evaluating

Produce several genuinely different directions before judging any of them. Different
means different underlying approach — not the same study with the population or the
instrument swapped, which is how a list of five ideas turns out to be one idea.

Deliberately include a direction the writer would not have reached from where they
started: a different method, a different level of analysis, a different discipline's
framing of the same problem.

Then stop generating and start evaluating. Doing both at once kills the weaker ideas
before they are developed enough to judge, and those are sometimes the ones worth having.

## Do not hand over a winner

Rank if the writer asks, but the choice stays theirs. Give the trade-offs — what each
direction would cost, what it would rule out, what has to be true for it to work — rather
than a verdict. A number attached to an idea at this stage is a way of organising
judgement, never a measurement, and presenting it as one is false precision.

Keep the rejected options and the reason each was set aside. Rejected directions come
back, usually when the chosen one stalls.

## About "nobody has studied this"

Almost always false, and the fastest way for a proposal to lose credibility with anyone
who knows the field.

Say what you actually know: "tidak menemukan penelitian yang membahas ini secara
langsung" — and state what was searched and what was not. When web research is switched
on, search before making any claim about a gap. When it is off, say the gap is unverified
rather than asserting one.

A real gap is specific: this population, this condition, this measurement. A gap that
covers a whole field means the search was too shallow.

## Pressure-test before committing

Once a direction is chosen, attack it deliberately — not to reject it, but to find where
it breaks while the cost of changing course is still low:

- What would have to be true for this to fail entirely?
- What is the most likely alternative explanation for the expected result?
- Would a null result still be worth reporting? If not, that is a design problem, not an
  outcome risk.
- Is the data, the access, the equipment, the population, and the time actually
  obtainable — by this writer, not in principle?
- Does it need ethical approval, and how long does that take?

Feasibility and ethics are gates, not scoring factors. A brilliant direction that cannot
be executed by this person, in this time, with this access, is not the strongest option;
it is the one that produces an abandoned project.
