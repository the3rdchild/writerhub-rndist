# Research proposals

Writing to be funded. A proposal is not a small paper about work you have not done yet —
it is an argument that this work is worth money and that you are the one to do it.

Adapted from `research-grants` in K-Dense-AI/scientific-agent-skills (MIT). See NOTICE.

## The funder's own document outranks everything here

Every funder scores against its own published criteria, and those criteria change between
calls. Do not state a specific page limit, budget ceiling, deadline, or required section
from memory — get them from the call document itself, from the writer, or from web
research if it is switched on in the conversation.

The upstream source for this skill describes American agencies in detail. Do not carry
those specifics over to a different funder: what NSF calls broader impacts, another body
may not ask for at all, or may ask for under a name that means something else. Ask which
funder and which scheme before giving concrete guidance.

What follows is the part that holds across funders.

## Four questions every reviewer is answering

Whatever the scoring sheet is called, it resolves to these:

**Does this matter?** If the work succeeds, what changes — for the field, for practice,
for the people affected. Not "this topic is important" but "this specific gap is blocking
this specific thing".

**Is it actually new?** New to this field, not new to the applicant. An incremental step
described in ambitious language is the most common failure, and reviewers who know the
field see through it immediately.

**Will it work?** The methods must be detailed enough to judge, the timeline must be
possible, and the scope must fit the money and the years. Overpromising reads as
inexperience, not ambition — a reviewer who thinks the plan cannot be delivered scores it
down however exciting it is.

**Can these people do it?** Track record, team composition, facilities, access to the
data or the site or the population. A gap here is best answered with a collaborator, not
with confidence.

Address each explicitly. Proposals lose points for leaving a criterion to be inferred,
even when the answer is obvious from the text.

## How reviewers actually read

Assume a reviewer with real expertise but not in this exact niche, reading late, holding
a stack of others. That produces concrete rules:

- The opening must carry the whole argument. Many reviewers form a view in the first page
  and spend the rest confirming it.
- Objectives must be specific and checkable. "Understand the mechanism of X" is not an
  objective; a reviewer cannot tell whether you achieved it.
- Every objective needs a method attached, and every method a way to tell whether it
  worked. Say what happens if the primary approach fails — a stated fallback reads as
  competence, not doubt.
- The budget is part of the argument. Each line justified by an activity in the plan;
  anything not traceable to the work looks like padding and casts doubt on the rest.
- Jargon that a neighbouring-field reviewer cannot parse costs points silently.

## Where proposals are actually lost

Mostly not on the science:

- Applying to the wrong scheme, or to a call whose priorities the proposal ignores.
- Exceeding a limit, missing a required attachment, or submitting late — none of which
  are read past.
- Objectives so broad that feasibility cannot be assessed.
- No preliminary evidence where the scheme expects it.
- A team with a visible hole in it and no collaborator to fill it.

## On resubmission

If the proposal was declined and the reviews came with it, those reviews are the
specification for the next attempt. Address them explicitly, and say what changed.
Resubmitting a near-identical proposal to a panel that partly remembers the last one
fails twice.
