# Venue rules

Preparing a document for somewhere that will reject it on formatting alone — a journal, a
conference, a funder, a faculty.

Adapted from `venue-templates` in K-Dense-AI/scientific-agent-skills (MIT). See NOTICE.

## The rule that governs everything else

**You do not know this venue's current rules, and neither does the writer's memory of
them.** Page limits change, anonymity requirements change, section requirements change,
and they change between one call and the next at the same venue.

So never state a specific limit, margin, font size, or section list from your own
knowledge. Every concrete requirement comes from one of two places:

1. the venue's own current instructions — the call for papers, the author guidelines, the
   funder's document, which the writer can fetch or paste in; or
2. the writer telling you.

When neither is available, say what needs to be looked up and where, then help with
everything that does not depend on it. A confidently stated page limit that turns out to
be last year's is worse than no answer, because nobody checks a number that sounds sure.

If web research is switched on in the conversation, use it to fetch the current
instructions and cite what you found.

## Formatting is the template's job, not yours

Once the rules are known, they are applied through `apply_template_format` and the page
setup tools — not by describing them in prose and hoping. If the venue's format is one the
template catalogue already covers, that catalogue wins over anything written here.

Your work is the part a template cannot do: deciding what the rules require, and adapting
the writing to them.

## What actually gets papers desk-rejected

Nearly all of it is mechanical, checked before anyone reads the science:

- Over the length limit. Note what counts toward it — references, figures, and appendices
  are sometimes excluded and sometimes not.
- Anonymity broken under blind review. Author names left in, an acknowledgements section
  that names the funder, "in our previous work [12]" with [12] being the authors' own,
  institutional details in the methods, or identifying metadata in the file itself.
- A required section missing entirely — a data availability statement, an ethics
  declaration, a conflicts statement, a broader-impacts section.
- The reference style not matching, or citations and reference list disagreeing.
- Figures unreadable at print size, or in the wrong format.
- The wrong track, category, or funding mechanism.

Check these before assessing the prose. A perfect argument three pages over the limit is
not read at all.

## Adapting the writing, not just the layout

Length limits are editorial decisions, not trimming exercises. When a document must lose
a third of its length, decide what the argument can survive without — usually background
the audience already has, and methodological detail that belongs in supplementary
material. Do not shrink every section proportionally; that damages the whole document
evenly instead of protecting its centre.

Audience differs by venue. A general-science readership needs the significance stated
early and the jargon carried; a specialist venue finds that same framing naive. The same
result is written differently for each, and this is not padding.

Where a venue requires a structured abstract, a plain-language summary, or a significance
statement, those are separate compositions with their own rules — not the introduction
cut down.
